Why heterogeneous nucleation needs less energy

  1. The critical radius is the same. r* = 2γSL/ΔGv is set by the curvature of the solid–liquid interface, so the dotted r* line passes through both maxima.
  2. Far fewer atoms have to cluster. On the substrate the nucleus is only a cap of that sphere. Its volume, and the number of atoms that must come together by a random fluctuation, is f(θ) of the full sphere (the dashed ghost on the right).
  3. The substrate pays part of the interface bill. The cap removes some high-energy mold–liquid interface (γML) and replaces it with lower-energy mold–solid interface (γSM). Both the surface cost and the volume benefit scale by f(θ), so ΔG*het = f(θ)·ΔG*hom.
  4. The rate goes as exp(−ΔG*/kT). Shrinking the barrier by a factor f changes the nucleation probability by many orders of magnitude. That is why real melts freeze on walls, inclusions and inoculants at a few kelvin of undercooling, not the hundreds of kelvin homogeneous nucleation needs.
